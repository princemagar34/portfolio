Genjutsu

An AI-powered phishing detection and honeypot system for Gmail, Outlook and any IMAP mailbox. It scans every incoming email through nine layers of checks, flags dangerous messages in real time, and replies to scammers with an AI decoy that draws out their intent.

  • Gmail API & Microsoft Graph (OAuth 2.0), IMAP
  • SPF / DKIM / DMARC
  • Naive Bayes classifier
  • NLP signals
  • Google Safe Browsing
  • URL & QR code scanning
  • Attachment content scanning
  • Shared threat feed
  • “Nami” AI honeypot
  • Telegram alerts

Detect

Every incoming mail is checked for spoofed senders, poisoned links, hidden QR codes, risky attachments and scam language, then scored.

Deceive

Nami, a GPT-OSS-120B persona on Groq, replies to scammers and wastes their time while the system learns from them.

Govern

A three-role admin portal with organization dashboards and PDF reports, running on Render, Cloudflare Workers and PostgreSQL.

Inside Genjutsu

How a suspicious email moves through the system, what testing showed, and how the results reach a SOC.

The pipeline

  1. Sender: SPF, DKIM and DMARC parsed from the real headers, plus look-alike domain checks.
  2. Content: phishing and urgency wording, NLP signals and a Naive Bayes classifier trained on scam and safe mail.
  3. Links: pattern checks and Google Safe Browsing, including links hidden in QR codes.
  4. Attachments: double extensions, risky file types and what is hidden inside the files.
  5. Shared intelligence: a global block list and a threat feed of attackers seen across Genjutsu users.

The final score decides what happens next: move to spam, alert on Telegram, or hand the sender to Nami.

Case study: phish_lab

A credential-harvesting attack I built from scratch for my Ethical Hacking coursework, run end to end, then turned around to show how defenders catch it.

The attack chain

  1. Recon: the target and their email address were found through open-source intelligence.
  2. Weaponise: Flask and Jinja2 clones of the Instagram, Facebook and Snapchat login pages, served over HTTPS through ngrok and hidden behind a shortened link.
  3. Deliver: a "suspicious login" warning sent with the Social-Engineer Toolkit, posing as Instagram Security.
  4. Exploit: a double-capture trick rejects the first password, so the victim types it twice. Both appear live on the attacker's dashboard.
  5. Take over: the stolen credentials log in and change the password, locking the owner out with no malware at all.

How a SOC catches it

  1. At the mail gateway: a "security alert" whose sender fails DMARC for the brand it claims, carrying a shortened link to a tunnelling domain.
  2. In identity logs: a sign-in from a new IP and device, followed within minutes by a password change, is a classic takeover pattern.
  3. With users: a login page that rejects a correct password once and then accepts it is a tell people can be trained to notice.

The full write-up also covers the legal side under Nepal's Electronic Transactions Act 2063. Genjutsu was built to stop exactly this kind of email.
